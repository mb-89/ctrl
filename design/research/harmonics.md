## Reviewer's corrections

- Dead-time 6th harmonic is mostly on the d axis, not q. The dead-time error vector follows the current vector, so its 6·fe ripple lies mostly perpendicular to the current. With i_d = 0 that perpendicular direction is the d axis. In the rotor frame the d-axis 6th-harmonic voltage is (4V_d/π)(1/5 + 1/7) ≈ 0.21 V, and the torque-producing q axis gets only (4V_d/π)(1/5 − 1/7) ≈ 0.035 V. I checked this numerically with V_d = 0.48 V: d 0.2095 V, q 0.0349 V. The claimed '0.2 V of 6th-harmonic q-voltage → 0.2 A → ~0.01 N·m' is therefore about 6× (15 dB) too high. The direct estimate is ~0.035 A → ~0.002 N·m, about 0.7 % rather than 2–4 %. The 'more physical alternative' of injecting 0.2·cos(6pθ) V at the armature has the same flaw: inject ~0.035 V, or note that most of the effect is on the d axis, which the DC-motor model does not have. Tr6 = 0.005 N·m is fine as a demo value, but not with this derivation.
- Sign error in the sin/cos offset term. With us = sinθ + cs and uc = cosθ + cc, θm − θ ≈ cs·cosθ − cc·sinθ, not 'cc·sinθ − cs·cosθ'. Numerical check at θ = 0.7: numeric −0.00512, correct formula −0.00524, research formula +0.00524. The amplitude-mismatch term −(k/2)·sin2θ and the non-orthogonality term (δ/2)(1 − cos2θ) are correct. The error only shifts the phase φ1.
- 'A falling line is impossible for a real order, which is how you recognise an alias' is too strong. Physical sidebands such as f_PWM − k·fe and f_s − k·f_rot also fall during a run-up whenever the measurement bandwidth includes the carrier. The research's own 1 kHz ZOH images at 1000 − f are physical in the continuous signal. A falling line means a fold or a carrier sideband. The reliable test is the one the research gives elsewhere: change the sample rate or point count and see whether the line moves.
- 'Tektronix recommends Sample mode for revealing aliasing' is not supported by the cited Tek page. That page only says Hi Res 'is also an anti-aliasing filter' and points to Peak Detect for finding lost samples. Older Tek TDS literature links Peak Detect, not Sample, to revealing aliasing. Tek's application note does say Hi Res 'may reduce some aliased frequencies… others may still be present', which supports the research's sinc caveat. Cite that note instead.
- The Allegro AN126 numbers (0.6°/1 % offset, 0.29°/0.01 sync, 0.5°/° non-orthogonality) are slopes from the note's analytical model (its Figs. 4b–6b and Table 1), not measurements. The title is 'Minimizing Angle Error in 2D Magnetic Sensors by Nonorthogonality Correction', and the figures apply to 2D TMR magnetic sensors.
- The arXiv 2307.11868 source has been withdrawn by its author, and its abstract page does not give V_DC·t_d/T_s. Replace it with a textbook or other standard reference for the average dead-time error V_d = t_d·f_PWM·V_dc, which is a standard result in any case.
- Joksimovic/Levi/Vukosavic appeared in IEEE TIE vol. 70, no. 5, pp. 4472–4484 (2023), early access 2022 (DOI 10.1109/TIE.2022.3189068), not 'IEEE TIE 2022'.
- The MathWorks page is titled 'Suppress PMSM Harmonics Using Adaptive Notch Filter'. It finds the notch frequency from FFTs at an operating point. It is not a speed-tracking notch at f = k·p·ω̂/2π, so 'as in MathWorks' PMSM example' overstates the precedent for a tracking notch through a run-up.
- 'The DC-motor structure is exactly the q-axis model of a surface PMSM under FOC with i_d = 0' needs caveats. With the amplitude-invariant transform Kt = 1.5·p·ψ while Ke = p·ψ, so Kt ≠ Ke. 'Exactly' also assumes a current controller holds i_d ≡ 0. In real FOC the speed PI commands i_q, not voltage, and the inner current loop largely rejects dead-time voltage ripple wherever 6·fe is well below the current-loop bandwidth.
- 'A digital filter applied to already-sampled scope data cannot remove an alias' is worded too strongly. A notch at the folded frequency does attenuate it. What post-sampling filtering cannot do is tell an alias from genuine content at the same frequency, or undo the fold. Also, a fixed 150 Hz notch takes two bites out of the 6F line, not one: once on the way up at 2.08 s and again on the folded descent at (400 − 150)/72 = 3.47 s.
- 'A constant-speed chirp example started from standstill saturates the current' should say the voltage. The model has only a ±24 V limit and no current limit.
- Rec 1 says this is 'the not perpendicular the person means'. That is an interpretation, not established. Present it as the most plausible physical reading.

## Missing (per the reviewer)

- The sin/cos-error to 1F/2F mapping only holds for single-period sensors: a 1X resolver or an on-axis/side-shaft magnetic angle sensor. A p_r-speed resolver puts them at p_r and 2·p_r. HEIDENHAIN separates the 'position error within one signal period' from the graduation and eccentricity error over one revolution. The example notes should say this, or the German explanation of Orthogonalitätsfehler will be wrong for optical encoders.

## Summary

The demo is a speed loop with the encoder in the feedback path and a speed ramp from 0 to 3600 rpm. The encoder error is a 1F term (eccentricity: θm = θ + e1·sin(θ+φ1)) plus a 2F term (the sin/cos channels are not at exactly 90°, or amplitudes or field shape are mismatched: e2·sin(2θ+φ2)). The 6F torque ripple is 6× the electrical frequency (dead time puts 5th/7th harmonics on the phases, and in the rotor frame they become one 6th): Tr·sin(6·p·θ+φ6). With pole pairs p = 1 it is mechanical order 6. On a spectrogram of the speed estimate with a 2000-point scope (fs = 400 Hz, Nyquist 200 Hz), orders 1 and 2 are bright rays from the origin. The 6F ray reaches Nyquist at 2.78 s, folds back and ends at 40 Hz. I checked this in a plain-JS simulation (/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-ctrl/281fed85-1c0b-52ae-b29a-1fd26dd76401/scratchpad/sim/enc.mjs, with PNG spectrograms in the same folder); the line levels quoted below come from it. One change to the brief: a 1 kHz speed loop does not divide evenly into the scope's 2.5 ms sample period. The images of the controller's zero-order hold then fold into extra lines mirrored at 200 − f. Use Ts = 0.5 ms (2 kHz, a typical servo speed-loop rate) so the scope rate divides the controller rate exactly. In a German write-up, 'not perpendicular' is best explained as the Orthogonalitätsfehler of the sin/cos channels. A tilted optical disc also gives 2F, but only α²/4, which is negligible.

## Recommendations

### 1. Encoder model: 1F eccentricity and 2F non-orthogonality as one Function block

**What.** Measured angle θm = θ + e1·sin(θ+φ1) + e2·sin(2θ+φ2), computed in a Function block from the true (unwrapped) rotor angle θ. 1F comes from eccentricity (graduation or magnet centre offset from the rotation axis) or from DC offsets in the sin/cos channels. 2F comes from non-orthogonality of the sin/cos channels (the 'not perpendicular' the person means) or from amplitude mismatch. In magnetic encoders, tilt or displacement that changes field ellipticity also gives 2F.

**How.** Function block formula: `u + 0.02*sin(u + 0.3) + 0.01*sin(2*u + 1.1)` (e1 = 0.02 rad = 1.15°, e2 = 0.01 rad = 0.57°). Physics to put in the example notes. (a) Eccentricity e of a disc of radius R read by one head: Δθ = (e/R)·sin(θ+φ) rad. That is HEIDENHAIN's ±412·e/D arcsec with e in µm and D in mm, about 5″ per µm on a typical angle encoder. Magnetic side-shaft: 2e/r rad (MPS AN142). (b) Sin/cos channels us = sinθ + cs, uc = (1+k)·cos(θ+δ) + cc, θm = atan2(us,uc). To first order: offset gives cc·sinθ − cs·cosθ (1F); amplitude mismatch k gives −(k/2)·sin2θ (2F); non-orthogonality δ gives (δ/2)·(1 − cos2θ) (2F plus a constant). Allegro AN126 measured 0.6°/1 % offset (1st), 0.29°/1 % mismatch (2nd) and 0.5°/° non-orthogonality (2nd). (c) Mechanical 'not perpendicular': a tilted optical disc gives Δθ ≈ (α²/4)·sin2θ, only 0.25 µrad at α = 1 mrad, so negligible. Angular misalignment β through a single-joint coupling gives the Cardan error tanθ2 = tanθ1/cosβ, that is (β²/4)·sin2θ, 2F, 1.9 mrad at β = 5°. Typical magnitudes: resolver about ±10 arcmin (TI), ±3…30 arcmin (Hengstler); AS5047D ±0.8° at optimum placement, ±1.2° with 0.5 mm displacement over temperature; optical arcseconds. The demo values are exaggerated compared with optical encoders but realistic for an uncalibrated magnetic encoder. An optional, more physical variant is `atan2(sin(u)+cs, (1+k)*cos(u+d)+cc)`. It wraps to ±π, so the speed estimator must then unwrap.

**Why.** In the speed estimate, an angle error of order k becomes a speed error of amplitude k·e_k·Ω. These lines get brighter with speed (+6 dB per doubling), the opposite of mechanical ripple. That makes them easy to identify, and it is a real effect that drive engineers see.

### 2. 6F torque ripple: electrical order 6, mechanical order 6p

**What.** Add T_r = Tr6·sin(6·p·θ + φ6) to the shaft torque from a Function block on θ, wired to the motor's load-torque input. Dead time (and 5th/7th back-EMF harmonics) puts 5th harmonics (negative sequence) and 7th harmonics (positive sequence) on the stator currents. In the rotor (dq) frame both land on 6·fe, so i_q and torque ripple at 6·fe = 6·p·f_rot.

**How.** Function formula: `0.005*sin(6*1*u)` (Tr6 = 0.005 N·m, 2 % of a 0.25 N·m rating; p = 1 so the line is order 6). Expose p as a knob: with p = 2 the line is order 12 and zig-zags across the spectrogram. Physics for the notes: per phase, ΔV = V_d·sgn(i) with V_d ≈ T_d·f_PWM·V_dc, and its Fourier series is (4V_d/π)[cosφ + (1/5)cos5φ − (1/7)cos7φ …] (Joksimovic/Levi/Vukosavic, IEEE TIE 2022). Example: 1 µs × 20 kHz × 24 V = 0.48 V. That gives about 0.2 V of 6th-harmonic q-voltage, which through the 1 Ω armature means about 0.2 A, or about 0.01 N·m ripple at low speed. So Tr6 = 0.005–0.01 N·m is realistic. A more physical alternative injects `0.2*cos(6*p*u)` V at the armature voltage instead of a torque; the ripple then falls with speed through 1/(R + j6ωeL). Confounders worth a footnote: a current-sensor offset gives 1·fe ripple and a gain mismatch gives 2·fe ripple (Chung & Sul 1998). Cogging torque appears at mechanical order LCM(slots, 2p).

**Why.** The person said '6F'. Drive literature counts in electrical orders, so the scope must label mechanical orders and state f = 6·p·f_rot. Otherwise a p = 4 motor would surprisingly show order 24.

### 3. Block diagram and parameter set (verified in simulation)

**What.** Speed reference r is a ramp. A Controller block holds a PI (Ts = 0.5 ms) acting on (r, ω̂). The motor is armature plus inertia with a load-torque port. An Integrator gives θ. One Function block makes the ripple (θ → load port), another the encoder (θ → θm). A discrete backward difference gives ω̂. The DC-motor structure is exactly the q-axis model of a surface PMSM under FOC with i_d = 0.

**How.** Motor: R = 1 Ω, L = 1 mH (armature firstOrder K = 1 A/V, τ = 1 ms), Kt = Ke = 0.05, J = 2e-5 kg·m², b = 1e-5 (mechanics firstOrder K = 1/b = 1e5, τ = J/b = 2 s), V limit ±24 V. Plant poles are −147 and −853 rad/s. PI: Kp = 0.068 V·s/rad, Ki = 10 V/rad (Ti = 6.8 ms cancels the mechanical pole), with conditional-integration anti-windup. Including the Ts delay this gives crossover 31 Hz and 72° phase margin. Speed estimate: ω̂[k] = (θm[k] − θm[k−1])/Ts. Either build it as a small 'Discrete derivative' block, or from existing blocks as Dead time(Ts) + Sum(+−) + Gain(1/Ts) ahead of the controller, which equals the backward difference at the sample instants. Reference: Ramp k = 75.4 rad/s² from 0 to 377 rad/s (3600 rpm, f_rot 0→60 Hz) in 5 s. Integration: RK4 with h = Ts/4 = 125 µs, 40,000 steps in a few milliseconds. h = Ts gave the same line levels to 0.2 dB. Probes: r, ω (true), ω̂, θm − θ, V, i, T_r. Results: peak V 19.7 V, peak i 0.79 A, final ω 380 rad/s.

**Why.** These numbers give an unsaturated, well-damped loop whose bandwidth (31 Hz) is close to the 1F/2F frequencies. The loop therefore turns sensor error into real motion: the true speed ω shows orders 1 and 2 at +12/+5 dB re 1 rad/s. Comparing the ω and ω̂ spectrograms is the teaching point.

### 4. 'Frequency sweep' = speed run-up; expected spectrogram (Campbell diagram)

**What.** A linear speed ramp from zero makes time proportional to speed, so the spectrogram is a Campbell diagram. Order k is a ray from the origin with slope k·(dΩ/dt)/2π = 12·k Hz/s. Fixed-frequency effects (structural resonance, PWM- or sample-rate-related tones) are horizontal lines and light up where an order crosses them (critical speeds). Aliasing: a line crossing Nyquist reflects as f_a = |f − fs·round(f/fs)|, so during a run-up it runs downward. A falling line is impossible for a real order, which is how you recognise an alias.

**How.** Simulated in ω̂ (dB re 1 rad/s, amplitude): orders 1 and 2 rise from −5/0 dB at 1 s to +19/+18 dB at 4.8 s. Order 6 is −5 dB at 1 s, reaches 200 Hz at t = 200/72 = 2.78 s (f_rot = 33 Hz), folds and ends at 400 − 360 = 40 Hz, at −18…−22 dB. It crosses order 2 at 4.17 s and order 1 at 4.76 s. Weak orders 3 and 4 (−17…−30 dB) come from intermodulation: the encoder error depends on the true angle, which the loop modulates. They are real, not artefacts. The noise floor is about −35…−45 dB. With p = 2, order 12 folds at 1.39 s, reaches 0 Hz at 2.78 s, rises to 200 Hz at 4.17 s and ends at 80 Hz. Second example variant (FFT mode, horizontal lines): constant 1500 rpm (start at that speed as an initial condition) plus a 0.01 N·m torque chirp from 1 to 400 Hz. Orders 1, 2 and 6 become horizontal lines at 25, 50 and 150 Hz. The chirp rises, reflects at 200 Hz at 2.5 s, and its brightness traces the loop's disturbance response. Optional resonance: a two-mass load (J_L = 2e-5, shaft stiffness 5.7 N·m/rad, f_res ≈ 120 Hz) gives a horizontal line that lights up when order 6 crosses it at 1.67 s.

**Why.** This is exactly what the person described: harmonics as rays from the origin and the 'spectral reflections' at the sample rate, with numbers checked in simulation.

### 5. Spectrogram algorithm and display settings

**What.** A short-time FFT (STFT) of the 2000 decimated scope samples, with the reference speed shown as the overlay on a second y-axis.

**How.** Hann window, N = 128 (0.32 s, Δf = 3.1 Hz), hop 8 (20 ms, 235 columns), zero-padded to 256, radix-2 FFT. Total cost is well under 5 ms. Remove a linear trend from each segment: the ramp in ω̂ otherwise leaks through the Hann sidelobes and hides the low orders. Amplitude scaling: |A| = 2|X_k|/Σw, shown in dB re 1 unit. Colour range: max − 65 dB to max, with a perceptual colormap. Window rule for a chirping line: T_opt ≈ 1/√(df/dt), which is 0.29 s for order 1 and 0.12 s for order 6. N = 128 is the best compromise here; offer 64/128/256. Overlay CH1 as r/2π in Hz on the second axis so the order-1 line sits exactly on it. Optionally draw folded order guides f_k(t) = fold(k·p_k·r(t)/2π, fs) for k = 1, 2, 6. FFT mode over a run-up smears every order into a plateau from 0 to k·60 Hz, so suggest constant speed or a time-gated window for FFT mode.

**Why.** With these settings all lines are visible in the verified run. Without per-segment detrending, the strongest component, the ramp itself, swamps the bottom of the plot.

### 6. Make aliasing a controllable, explainable effect

**What.** Give the person knobs that separate scope (instrument) aliasing from aliasing in the control loop, as on a real digital scope.

**How.** (1) Scope acquisition 'Sample' versus 'Hi-Res'. Sample keeps the instantaneous value every 2.5 ms, so aliases are visible; Tektronix recommends Sample mode for revealing aliasing. Hi-Res averages the fine simulation samples in each interval. Tek calls this an anti-alias filter, but it is only a sinc: measured −4 to −12 dB on the folded 6F line, not removal. (2) A scope point count of 2000 or 4000: an alias moves when the point count changes, a real line does not. (3) Controller Ts. At 0.5 ms there is no aliasing in the loop for p = 1. At Ts = 2.5 ms (400 Hz) the 6F speed ripple aliases inside the controller, which then drives real motion at the folded frequency. This showed in true ω about 27 dB below the 6F line and stays when the scope samples at 1 kHz. At Ts = 1 ms with a 2.5 ms scope, the zero-order-hold images at 1000 ± f fold to 200 − f, giving mirrored lines; this is the controller's 'sample-rate reflection'. (4) p = 1, 2 or 4 (order 24 reaches 1440 Hz and also aliases inside a 2 kHz loop).

**Why.** The person asked to see 'spectral reflections from the sampling rate'. Engineers need to know which reflections are instrument artefacts that a better scope removes, and which are physical effects a filter cannot remove.

### 7. Live filters in FFT/spectrogram mode: what they can and cannot do

**What.** Explain in the example that a digital filter applied to already-sampled scope data cannot remove an alias, because it now sits at a legitimate in-band frequency. A filter in the diagram, acting before sampling, can. Order lines move with speed, so a fixed notch removes only a horizontal line.

**How.** Use a low-pass (for example 80 Hz) on ω̂ to show orders being cut. Provide a tracking notch at f = k·p·ω̂/2π (adaptive notch in the dq frame, as in MathWorks' PMSM example) to remove 6F throughout the run-up. A fixed notch at 150 Hz only takes a short bite out of the 6F ray.

**Why.** This keeps the planned live-filter feature honest and makes for a good teaching moment.

## Pitfalls

- A 1 kHz controller with a 2000-point / 5 s scope (2.5 ms) is not commensurate. The zero-order-hold images at 1000 ± f fold to 200 − f and produce extra lines mirrored at 100 Hz (seen in simulation). Fix: make the controller rate an integer multiple of the scope rate (Ts = 0.5, 1.25 or 2.5 ms), or change the scope point count (2500 or 5000 for 1 kHz).
- The integration step must divide the scope period (125 µs × 20 = 2.5 ms). Otherwise sampling-instant jitter adds sidebands.
- Do not wrap θm to [0, 2π) unless the speed estimator unwraps: Δ = mod(Δ + π, 2π) − π. A missed wrap is a 2π/Ts spike (12.6 krad/s) once per revolution and turns into a comb of all orders.
- Without per-segment detrending (or a high-pass), the ramp's leakage hides orders 1 and 2 at low frequency.
- Encoder quantization of 14 bits at Ts = 0.5 ms raises the floor to about −22 dB and buries the folded 6F line (simulated). Keep quantization off by default and make it a knob.
- Label orders as mechanical. '6F' in drive literature is 6× electrical = 6p mechanical; for p = 1 they coincide, for p = 4 the line is order 24.
- With p = 1, current-sensor offset (1·fe) and gain mismatch (2·fe) ripple fall exactly onto the encoder's 1F and 2F lines. Do not model them in the same example, or explain the overlap.
- 'Not perpendicular' as optical disc tilt gives only (α²/4)·sin2θ, which is negligible. Do not present tilt as the source of a visible 2F on an optical encoder. Use sin/cos non-orthogonality, amplitude mismatch, magnetic-field ellipticity, or coupling (Cardan) misalignment.
- Encoder error is amplified by k·Ω in ω̂, and the PI loop turns it into real speed ripple (true ω shows orders 1 and 2 at about +12/+5 dB). With much larger e1 or speed, V saturates; peak V was 19.7 V of 24 V.
- Hi-Res (boxcar averaging) only attenuates aliases by a sinc (nulls at multiples of fs). It does not remove them, so do not call it an anti-alias filter without that caveat.
- A constant-speed chirp example started from standstill saturates the current (step to 1500 rpm). Start at speed as an initial condition, or ramp first.
- Typical torque-ripple percentages from dead time (about 2–4 % here) are an engineering estimate from V_d = T_d·f_PWM·V_dc and the armature impedance, not a sourced figure.
