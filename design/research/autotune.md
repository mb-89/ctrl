# Research: autotune

Reader: whoever implements this part of Loop Tuner. Gathered by a research agent and checked by a skeptical reviewer on 2026-10-24; the reviewer's corrections are listed first and override the findings where they disagree.

## Reviewer's corrections

- Describing-function error is stated the wrong way round. The research says the relay 'overestimates Ku by up to 27% for delay-dominated plants … it is accurate for lag-dominated plants'. The +27% (4/π) for a pure delay is right. For lag-dominated FOPDT and integrator+delay plants, though, y is nearly triangular and the DF underestimates Ku by about 19% (factor 8/π²≈0.81). A relay simulation of e^{-s}/(Ts+1) gave Ku_DF/Ku_true = 0.97 at T/L=0.5, 0.89 at T/L=1, 0.83 at 5 and 0.81 at 20–100. So the error runs from about −19% to +27%. The DF is close only near intermediate τ or for higher-order lags with near-sinusoidal output. Also, the relay period differs from Pu by a few % (2.62 vs 2.75 at T/L=0.5). So the DFT gives an exact G(jω_osc), but at a phase that is not exactly −180°. Use its measured phase, as in phase-margin placement, rather than taking |G| as K180.
- SIMC margins for τc=θ are overstated as 'Ms≈1.6, GM≈3, PM≈60°'. The full FOPDT range is GM 2.96–3.14, PM 46.9°–61.4°, Ms 1.59–1.70. The 61.4°/1.59 end holds for τ1≤8θ; the integrating end is GM 2.96, PM 46.9°, Ms 1.70. I checked this numerically and against Skogestad's NPC07 paper, which gives GM 2.96–3.14 and Ms 1.70–1.59.
- AMIGO step rule for integrating plants with PID: the research copies Advanced PID Control eq. 7.8, 'K=0.45/Kv'. That is dimensionally inconsistent. The T→∞ limit of eq. 7.7, (1/Kp)(0.2+0.45T/L) with Kp/T=Kv, gives K=0.45/(Kv·L), which matches the PI rule 0.35/(Kv·L) in eq. 7.6. Implement 0.45/(Kv·L). Ti=8L and Td=0.5L are correct.
- The cost does not 'mirror' Grimholt & Skogestad 2018. Their IAE°_dy and IAE°_du are fixed weights: the IAEs of IAE-optimal reference PI controllers with Ms=1.59 (their Table 1). They use output-disturbance and input-disturbance steps, with no setpoint or prefilter test. Normalising by the starting controller instead makes the optimum depend on p0, because the effective weights change with the start. If the start is unstable or very poor, a normaliser blows up and that term's weight goes to about 0; if the start has TV_u≈0, the term degenerates. Freeze the normalisers from a fixed reference (for example a relay/AMIGO or SIMC baseline) and show '% of start' only as a display ratio. Also, with w_r=w_d=0.5 and w_u=0.1, J(start)=1.1, not 1.
- Simulation cost is about 10× higher than the '~1 ms per evaluation' assumed. I timed the repo's own simulateSystem on the normalised System-library entries (2000 samples, Node, warmed up): conical tank ≈6.6 ms, pendulum swing-up ≈7.9 ms, DC motor ≈10.5 ms, mass–spring ≈11.4 ms, triple pendulum ≈18.5 ms per run. So NM with 300 evaluations takes ≈2–6 s, CMA-ES with 200–1000 evaluations ≈2–20 s, the 25×25 grid ≈4–12 s, and the robustness scenarios multiply all of these. 'Finishes in under a second' and 'grid ≈0.5–1 s' are wrong. Time-slicing or a Web Worker with progressive display is needed. This also weakens the cost-based argument for dismissing sample-efficient methods; code complexity remains the valid reason.
- The DC-motor pitfall does not match the v2 library. The motor in src/core/plants.js has an armature lag (τ=0.01 s) plus a mechanical lag with back-EMF: ω/V = 5/(0.002s²+0.21s+3.5), poles ≈ −20.8 and −84.2 rad/s. The position loop is therefore third order and crosses −180° at ≈42 rad/s (≈34 rad/s with the 5 ms ZOH half-sample), so Pu≈0.15–0.19 s and Ku≈50. The relay works on it. The 'k/(s(Ts+1)) only approaches −180°' case is v1's 'Motor position' example in src/core/examples.js. The real issue for the library motor is that Pu is only about 15–19 controller samples at Ts=10 ms: interpolate the switching times and use the DFT. Also, hysteresis cannot rescue a truly first-order loop, because −180°+arcsin(ε/a) only reaches −90° when ε=a.
- DARE by plain fixed-point iteration is not sub-millisecond in this setting. It converges linearly at a rate of about ρ(A_cl)². For a cart-pole with the proposed Bryson weights it took ≈900 iterations to reach 1e-10 at Ts=10 ms and ≈3400 at Ts=2.5 ms (checked with numpy). It gets slower still when the optimiser lowers weights and closed-loop poles move toward z=1. Inside a 1000-evaluation CMA-ES loop at n=8, make the structured doubling algorithm (or a warm-started solver) the default, not the alternative.
- The LQR parameterisation is inconsistent. The research says to keep R fixed 'otherwise one dimension is redundant', then proposes three groups: ρ in R=ρR0, a position weight and a rate weight. With Q=diag(q_p·Q_p, q_r·Q_r), scaling (q_p, q_r, ρ) together leaves K unchanged, so one of the three is redundant. Use, for example, ρ plus a position/rate ratio, or cart and angle weights with R fixed.
- 'Searching K directly does not work' is overstated. A local search started from a stabilising K, such as the existing LQR gains, with small steps stays inside the stabilising set. Gradient methods on K for LQR are known to converge (e.g. Fazel et al. 2018). Q/R search is preferable for interpretability and dimension, but it restricts K to gains that are LQR-optimal for the linear model, which may be suboptimal for the nonlinear, saturated plant (the cart is limited to ±25 N).
- The claims about v1 code are optimistic. tune.js and evaluateCandidate drive v1's slot loop (simulate with SLOTS and candidates), not the v2 diagram Controller block (simulateSystem). tune.js throws on 'vector' params, and the v2 variants (stateFeedback K, gainScheduled Kp/Ki tables, swingUp K) are vectors with no tunable flags in controllers.js. So 'NM already exists, the main work is the cost' understates the work: the tuner must be re-targeted to the diagram Controller block and must support vector entries. Also, penalized() is not strictly feasible-first. A feasible J=10 loses to an infeasible J=1 with violation 0.01, which scores 1+2·2=5; only compareEvals is strictly feasible-first. v1's restarts scale the step by 1/(round+1), not halving, and v1 starts with a 40-evaluation random scan.
- Bryson's-rule example: u_max=40 N contradicts the library, whose cart Controller limits u to ±25 N. Use the block's actual umin/umax.
- Minor points: (a) a Nelder–Mead shrink costs n new evaluations, not n+1. (b) Scenario robustness (nominal, gain ×0.7, gain ×1.4, extra delay) is at least 4× the simulations, not 3×. (c) The conical tank is self-regulating (outflow a√(2gh)), not integrating. (d) The Safonov–Athans margins of GM [1/2,∞) and PM ≥ 60° hold per input channel for diagonal R. (e) The default in Hansen's 2016 tutorial uses active (negative) weights for i>μ; the listed set is the positive-weights-only variant, which is fine but should be labelled as such.

## Missing (per the reviewer)

- Per-variant goals. The shared r-step/d-step cost does not measure swing-up quality (catch success, time to catch, energy error, cart travel within the track) or TVLQR trajectory tracking. 'Works for every Controller variant' only holds once these goals exist.
- MIGO, Åström–Hägglund's model-based design: maximise ki = Kp/Ti subject to Ms (or the combined M) ≤ 1.4 on the linearised loop, evaluated on a frequency grid. It is the source of the AMIGO rules, deterministic, far cheaper than simulation and fits the Analysis tab's Nyquist plot. It should sit beside the simulation optimiser once the design layer (linearisation) exists.
- The Ms/MT constraint depends on a linearisation layer (pending roadmap task 7, the design layer) that does not exist yet. The research should say that until then only scenario robustness is available.
- Industrial pattern-recognition autotuning (Foxboro EXACT; Kraus & Myron 1984) is missing. It iteratively adjusts PID from the overshoot and decay of closed-loop transients, a natural 'watch it improve' method and one of the main industrial autotuners besides the relay. The classic Ziegler–Nichols closed-loop gain sweep (raise Kp until sustained oscillation) is also visible, iterative and safe in simulation.
- VRFT (Campi, Lecchini & Savaresi 2002, Automatica 38:1337–1346) is the one-shot, data-driven counterpart to IFT and is worth a line in the deferred list.
- Relay on the nonlinear conical tank: the relay measures a describing function at amplitude a around one level, so a must be small compared with the operating point. The actuator range is asymmetric (umin=0, umax=3), which limits d near low flows; the 0.1 s pipe delay is what makes the relay oscillate.
- Compute budgeting and UI: given 7–20 ms per simulation, the UI needs a worker or per-frame budget, an evaluation counter and ETA, and should cap the CMA-ES and grid sizes, or use shorter or decimated test windows during the search with full-length validation afterwards.
- Ms on about 200 log-spaced points can miss narrow resonance peaks in lightly damped loops such as the pendulums; refine the grid around the maximum.
- v1 already has the metrics distIAE, uTV, saturation and itae in src/core/metrics.js; the cost can reuse them rather than redefine them.

## Summary

Build three methods. (1) An iterative optimiser over controller parameters against one normalised cost for following r, rejecting d and control effort, with limits on overshoot and robustness (Ms). This means v1's Nelder–Mead searching in log space (already in src/core/tune.js), plus CMA-ES for more than 4 parameters or rough costs. It is the only method that works for every Controller variant, and its cost-vs-iteration curve is the 'badly tuned system improves' story the person wants. (2) The Åström–Hägglund relay experiment: one closed-loop run gives Ku and Pu (better: the exact Nyquist point from a DFT of the limit cycle). A rule turns that into a controller, choosable as Ziegler–Nichols, Tyreus–Luyben, AMIGO or phase-margin placement. This is the industrial one-shot autotuner and gives the optimiser a good starting point, but it only works for stable or integrating SISO loops. (3) LQR weight tuning for the cart-pendulum family: start from Bryson's rule, search log-diagonal Q/R with the same optimiser, and recompute K by solving the DARE each time, so every candidate stabilises the linear model. Searching the gains K directly does not work because almost every random K is unstable. A step test with an FOPDT fit and a SIMC τc slider is a cheap optional fourth. IFT, extremum seeking and Bayesian optimisation are built for slow, expensive experiments on real hardware, so in a simulator that runs in milliseconds they add assumptions and tuning of the tuner without visible benefit; defer them.

## Recommendations

### 1. Goal/cost definition shared by all methods (r-following + d-rejection + effort, normalised, constrained)

**What.** Score each candidate from deterministic simulations with two windows: a servo test (step in r) and a regulator test (step in d at the plant input). Normalise each term by its value for the starting controller, so J(start) = 1 (show it as '% of start'). Overshoot, Ms and saturation are constraints, not cost terms.

**How.** Scenario: Sim A = r step Δr at t_r≈0.05·T_end, d=0; Sim B = input-load step Δd at t_d, r constant. Use one run with r at 0.05T and d at 0.5T only if the r response has settled before t_d. Fix the noise seed (common random numbers) so J is deterministic. Metrics on samples (dt=T/N, N=2000), e=r−y, all measured from the event time: IAE=Σ|e_k|dt; ISE=Σe_k²dt; ITAE=Σ(t_k−t_ev)|e_k|dt; effort TV_u=Σ|u_{k+1}−u_k| (catches noise amplification and derivative kick) or ∫(u−u_ss)²dt; OS=max(0,max_k sign(Δr)(y_k−r_end))/|Δr|; Usat=fraction of samples at the actuator limit. Cost: J(p)=w_r·IAE_r(p)/IAE_r(p0)+w_d·IAE_d(p)/IAE_d(p0)+w_u·TV_u(p)/TV_u(p0), defaults w_r=w_d=0.5, w_u=0.1. This mirrors Grimholt & Skogestad 2018: J=0.5(IAE_dy/IAE°_dy+IAE_du/IAE°_du) subject to Ms≤M_ub. Constraints: OS≤5–10 %; Ms≤1.6 (1.4 conservative, 2.0 aggressive). Compute Ms=max_ω 1/|1+L(e^{jωTs})| on about 200 log-spaced ω up to π/Ts, with the ZOH included in the linearised loop, and MT likewise. Ms guarantees GM≥Ms/(Ms−1) and PM≥2·arcsin(1/(2Ms)): Ms=1.6 gives GM≥2.67, PM≥36°; Ms=1.4 gives GM≥3.5, PM≥42°. If there is no linear model, measure robustness by scenario: worst J over the plant with gain ×0.7/×1.4 and extra delay Ts…2Ts (3× the simulations). Penalty: keep v1's feasible-first ranking and penalized() in src/core/goal.js, or use a smooth hinge J+10³·Σmax(0,g_i/scale_i)². Divergence: abort when |y|>10× range or NaN, and use cost =big·(2−t_div/T) (as v1 does) so the search can climb out. Two-degree-of-freedom structure (Åström): stage 1 tunes the feedback C (Kp,Ti,Td) on the d-window under the Ms bound; stage 2 tunes only the prefilter or setpoint weight b on the r-window. This removes the r/d conflict and keeps each stage at 1–3 parameters, which converges visibly. Choice of integral: ISE gives aggressive, oscillatory tunings; ITAE stresses settling; IAE is the standard compromise.

**Why.** Watching improvement only means something if the number is trustworthy. Normalising makes the weights dimensionless and the curve readable ('now 38 % of start'). Testing both r and d prevents tuning to one signal. The Ms bound avoids the classic trap that IAE-optimal loops are fragile (Ms 2–3), and separating feedback from prefilter matches the controller's existing prefilter.

### 2. Iterative optimiser: Nelder–Mead in log space (keep v1) + CMA-ES for higher-dimensional or non-smooth problems

**What.** A model-free search that uses only simulations. Every 'iteration' produces a new trial response and a best-so-far that only gets better. It works for PID with prefilter, gain-schedule tables, the swing-up energy gain and LQR weights.

**How.** Search variables x=log Kp, log Ti, log Td (or log Ki, log Kd), clamped to bounds; v1 already encodes them this way in /home/user/ctrl/src/core/tune.js. Nelder–Mead uses the standard coefficients reflection 1, expansion 2, contraction 0.5, shrink 0.5 (Lagarias et al. 1998) and an initial step of 0.7 in log space (about ×2). Restart with half the step when the simplex collapses (spread<1e-3). Stop when f_max−f_min≤1e-6(1+|f_min|). Typically 60–200 simulations for n=2–4; one display iteration = one NM step (1–2 simulations, n+1 on a shrink). Use CMA-ES for n≥4 or non-smooth costs (swing-up, 8–9 LQR weights, gain-schedule tables), following Hansen's tutorial defaults: λ=4+⌊3 ln n⌋ (n=3→7, n=9→10), μ=⌊λ/2⌋, w_i∝ln((λ+1)/2)−ln i normalised, μ_eff=1/Σw_i², c_σ=(μ_eff+2)/(n+μ_eff+5), d_σ=1+2·max(0,√((μ_eff−1)/(n+1))−1)+c_σ, c_c=(4+μ_eff/n)/(n+4+2μ_eff/n), c_1=2/((n+1.3)²+μ_eff), c_μ=min(1−c_1, 2(μ_eff−2+1/μ_eff)/((n+2)²+μ_eff)), E‖N(0,I)‖≈√n(1−1/(4n)+1/(21n²)), σ0≈0.5 in log space. Use a Jacobi eigendecomposition of C every generation, which is trivial for n≤10. 30–100 generations means 200–1000 simulations, about 1 s. One display iteration = one generation; draw the λ trial responses faintly so the cloud visibly converges. Use a seeded RNG (v1's lcg) so runs are reproducible. For two free parameters, optionally precompute J on a 25×25 log grid (625 simulations, ≈0.5–1 s, sliced per frame) and draw a contour or heatmap with the optimiser's path. Compass/Hooke–Jeeves pattern search (2n polls, halve the step on failure) is a simpler deterministic alternative. Bayesian optimisation is not needed here.

**Why.** It is the only method that applies to every controller variant and to nonlinear plants. Each simulation takes milliseconds, so the whole run finishes in under a second and can be replayed at viewing speed. NM already exists in v1, so the main work is the new cost; CMA-ES covers the cases where NM stalls (multimodal swing-up, many LQR weights).

### 3. Relay feedback auto-tune (Åström–Hägglund 1984) with selectable follow-up rules

**What.** One closed-loop experiment replaces the controller with a relay around the operating point. It measures Ku and Pu, or better the exact frequency-response point. A rule then gives Kp, Ti, Td; the person can compare ZN, TL, AMIGO and phase-margin candidates side by side, and the result can seed the optimiser. Applies to stable and integrating SISO loops: motor speed and position, the conical tank. Not to the pendulum.

**How.** Experiment: u=u0+d·sign(e), e=r−y. With hysteresis ε, switch to +d when e>ε and to −d when e<−ε. u0 is the equilibrium input at the setpoint; d is 2–10 % of the actuator range; ε≈2× the peak noise (0 if noiseless). Run until two consecutive periods agree within 2 % (typically 3–6 periods), discard the first period, and allow a run longer than 5 s (≥6·Pu). Describing-function estimate: a=(y_max−y_min)/2, Pu=measured period, Ku=4d/(πa), ωu=2π/Pu. With hysteresis the oscillation sits where G(jω)=−(π/4d)(√(a²−ε²)+jε), i.e. at phase −180°+arcsin(ε/a). Recommended exact version: take first Fourier coefficients over an integer number of periods, Y1=(2/T_w)Σy_k e^{−jω t_k}dt and U1 likewise, then G(jω_osc)=Y1/U1 and K180=|G|. The DF formula overestimates Ku by 4/π (+27 %) for a pure-delay plant, whose output is square. Bias adaptation for integrating or asymmetric loops: once per period, u0 += 0.5·d·(t₊−t₋)/(t₊+t₋). Process gain Kp_proc (needed for AMIGO κ) comes from a small extra step or from two bias levels. Rules use the ideal/ISA form u=Kp[e+(1/Ti)∫e dt+Td·de/dt]; parallel form Ki=Kp/Ti, Kd=Kp·Td; derivative filter Td/N with N≈10. ZN 1942: P Kp=0.5Ku; PI Kp=0.45Ku, Ti=Pu/1.2 (Åström–Hägglund quote 0.4Ku, 0.8Pu); PID Kp=0.6Ku, Ti=0.5Pu, Td=0.125Pu. Tyreus–Luyben 1992: PI Kp=Ku/3.2, Ti=2.2Pu; PID Kp=Ku/2.2, Ti=2.2Pu, Td=Pu/6.3. AMIGO frequency rules (Advanced PID Control eqs 7.11/7.12, designed for Ms≈1.4, κ=K180/Kp_proc=1/(Ku·Kp_proc), valid for κ>0.2, i.e. not lag-dominant): PI Kp=0.16Ku, Ti=Pu/(1+4.5κ); PID Kp=(0.3−0.1κ⁴)Ku, Ti=0.6Pu/(1+2κ), Td=0.15(1−κ)Pu/(1−0.95κ). Phase-margin placement (Åström–Hägglund 1984; works with any measured point G(jω0)=|G|e^{jφ}): choose φm=45–60°, then argC=−180°+φm−φ, |C|=1/|G|, Kp=cos(argC)/|G|. For PID with Ti=4Td: ω0Td=(tan argC+√(tan²argC+1))/2; at ω0=ωu this reduces to Kp=Ku·cos φm. For PI it needs argC∈(−90°,0], then Ti=−1/(ω0·tan argC). AMIGO setpoint weight for PID: b=0 if τ=L/(L+T)≤0.5, else 1. Cost: 1 relay simulation + 1 verification simulation per rule. Visible on the scope: the limit cycle builds up, a and Pu are annotated, then each rule's closed-loop response is shown. Conical tank: repeat at 3 levels to fill the gain-scheduled PI table.

**Why.** It is the industrial-standard, model-free autotuner and the method the person will know. The limit cycle is physically visible, and Ku/Pu tie straight to the Nyquist plot and Wurzelortskurve view in the Analysis tab. It gives the optimiser a sensible starting point instead of an arbitrary one.

### 4. LQR weight tuning for state-feedback variants (Bryson start + optimiser over log Q/R)

**What.** For cart and 1–3 rod pendulums, search the LQR weights instead of the gains. Each candidate K=dlqr(Ad,Bd,Q,R) stabilises the linearised plant. The same r/d cost (move the cart, push the system) judges it on the nonlinear simulation.

**How.** Linearise at the upright equilibrium by central differences (step ≈1e-6·scale). Discretise with ZOH at the controller Ts using the augmented matrix exponential exp([[A,B],[0,0]]Ts) (Padé with scaling and squaring). Start from Bryson's rule: Q=diag(1/x_i,max²), R=diag(1/u_max²), e.g. cart 0.5 m, angles 5–10°, rates 1 m/s and 1 rad/s, u_max=40 N. Solve the DARE by iterating P←Q+AᵀPA−AᵀPB(R+BᵀPB)⁻¹BᵀPA until ‖ΔP‖<1e-10‖P‖, or use the structured doubling algorithm for speed; sub-millisecond for n≤8. Then K=(R+BᵀPB)⁻¹BᵀPA and reference gain Nbar=1/(C_x(I−Ad+BdK)⁻¹Bd), or the kr=K[0] form the code uses. Search variables: log10 multipliers on diag Q, with R fixed (otherwise one dimension is redundant). For clear visuals reduce to 2–3 groups: ρ in R=ρR0, a position weight and a rate weight. Use NM for ≤4 parameters, CMA-ES for 8–9. Also offer a 1-D ρ sweep: J(ρ) for ρ=10⁻³…10³ (25 simulations) plotted as a curve. Continuous LQR with full state guarantees GM∈[1/2,∞) and PM≥60° per input (Safonov & Athans 1977). Discrete-time, observer-based (Doyle 1978) or delayed loops lose these guarantees, so keep the robustness-by-variation check. The same approach serves the trajectory+TVLQR variant (constant Q/R along the trajectory, with a backward Riccati pass per evaluation).

**Why.** Searching the 4–8 entries of K directly is hopeless: almost every random K destabilises an inverted pendulum and the cost landscape is mostly 'unstable'. Q/R space keeps the search inside the stabilising set and the knobs mean something ('penalise angle more'). Marco et al. (ICRA 2016) tuned LQR weights this way on hardware, using Bayesian optimisation.

### 5. Auto-tune tab UI flow (watchable iterations, cost vs iteration, apply best)

**What.** A step-by-step tab: what to tune → goal → method → watch it run → compare → apply. Computation runs at full speed and is recorded; the display replays it at a chosen pace.

**How.** 1) Target: pick the Controller block and variant, tick the free parameters (default: all tunable), show current values and bounds. 2) Goal: r test (amplitude, time), d test (input step, time), sliders for the weights r/d/effort, constraints (OS max, Ms max, u limit). The start controller is the reference (J=100 %). Toggle: 'feedback for d first, then prefilter for r'. 3) Method: Relay experiment (d, ε, rule list → candidates), Step test + SIMC (τc slider), Optimise (NM/CMA-ES, budget, seed), and LQR weights when the variant is state feedback. 4) Run on the scope: start response as a grey ghost, current trial thin, best-so-far bold, faint population for CMA-ES. Small plots: cost vs iteration (log y; one dot per evaluation, red if infeasible; best-so-far as a staircase) and parameter traces vs iteration; optional J landscape with the path for two parameters. Controls: Run, Pause, Step, Speed (2–30 iterations/s), and a scrubber to replay history. v1's generator already yields after each evaluation, so record the history and play it back. 5) Result table, start vs best: J, IAE_r, IAE_d, OS, TV_u, Ms, GM/PM. Buttons: 'Apply best' (undoable), 'Keep as new variant' (so before and after can be flipped like Simulink variants), 'Revert'. 6) Validation: re-simulate the best on held-out signals (ramp or sine, other amplitude) and plant variations ±30 %, drawn as an envelope.

**Why.** It follows the product rule 'show little at a time'. Replay separates the millisecond computation from human viewing speed, so the person can see the iterations rather than an instant jump. Keeping the result as a variant fits the existing variant mechanism.

### 6. Optional cheap add-on: step test + FOPDT fit + SIMC (one-knob τc) and classic step-response rules

**What.** An open-loop step (closed-loop with P for integrating plants), a first-order-plus-delay fit, then model-based rules. Gives a model for the Analysis tab and one intuitive tuning knob.

**How.** FOPDT model K·e^{−Ls}/(Ts+1). Two-point fit: T=1.5(t63−t28), L=t63−T. Area method (noise-robust): A0=∫(y∞−y)dt per Δu, T_ar=A0/K=L+T, A1=∫₀^{T_ar}y dt, T=e·A1/K, L=T_ar−T. Or least squares by NM over (log K, log T, L), showing the model curve converge onto the data (visible iterations). Add Ts/2 to L for the ZOH. Integrating plant: y≈k'(t−L). SIMC (Skogestad 2003; ideal PI): Kp=(1/K)·T/(τc+L), Ti=min(T, 4(τc+L)); τc=L means 'tight' (Ms≈1.6, GM≈3, PM≈60°), larger τc means smoother. SOPDT (T1≥T2) in series form: Kp=(1/K)·T1/(τc+L), Ti=min(T1,4(τc+L)), Td=T2; convert to ideal form with f=1+Td/Ti: Kp'=Kp·f, Ti'=Ti·f, Td'=Td/f. Integrating k'e^{−Ls}/s: Kp=1/(k'(τc+L)), Ti=4(τc+L). Half rule for model reduction: split the largest neglected time constant half into the delay and half into the smallest retained time constant. iSIMC-PI (Grimholt & Skogestad 2018): Kp=(1/K)(T+L/3)/(τc+L), Ti=min(T+L/3, 4(τc+L)); iSIMC PID adds Td=L/3 (series form) with τc≈L/2. ZN open loop (reaction curve: R=max slope/Δu, a=R·L): P Kp=1/a; PI Kp=0.9/a, Ti=3L; PID Kp=1.2/a, Ti=2L, Td=0.5L. Cohen–Coon (r=L/T): PI Kp=(T/(KL))(0.9+r/12), Ti=L(30+3r)/(9+20r); PID Kp=(T/(KL))(4/3+r/4), Ti=L(32+6r)/(13+8r), Td=4L/(11+2r). Cohen–Coon is oscillatory; show it as historical. IMC/λ: PI Kp=T/(K(λ+L)), Ti=T; PID (Rivera, Morari & Skogestad 1986) Kp=(T+L/2)/(K(λ+L/2)), Ti=T+L/2, Td=TL/(2T+L). AMIGO step rules (Ms≈1.4): PI Kp=0.15/K+(0.35−LT/(L+T)²)·T/(KL), Ti=0.35L+13LT²/(T²+12LT+7L²), integrating: Kp=0.35/(Kv·L), Ti=13.4L; PID Kp=(1/K)(0.2+0.45T/L), Ti=L(0.4L+0.8T)/(L+0.1T), Td=0.5LT/(0.3L+T), integrating: Kp=0.45/Kv, Ti=8L, Td=0.5L. Cost: 1 step simulation + fit (≈100 cheap model evaluations) + 1 verification simulation.

**Why.** Very cheap, and SIMC with τc=θ is close to IAE-optimal for FOPDT processes. The τc slider is a one-knob tuning control engineers know. Not usable for unstable plants; FOPDT fits underdamped plants poorly.

### 7. Defer IFT, extremum seeking and Bayesian optimisation (designed for expensive real experiments)

**What.** Document them and maybe add later as educational or hardware modes, but do not build them first.

**How.** IFT (Hjalmarsson, Gunnarsson & Gevers 1994; Hjalmarsson et al. 1998). Criterion J(ρ)=(1/2N)Σ[(L_y(y_k(ρ)−y_d,k))²+λ(L_u u_k(ρ))²]. Per iteration: 2 experiments for a 1-DOF controller (normal run with r, then a gradient run with r−y¹ as reference), 3 for 2-DOF. Gradient estimate ∂y/∂ρ≈(1/C)(∂C/∂ρ)y²; update ρ_{i+1}=ρ_i−γ_i R_i⁻¹∇̂J with the Gauss–Newton R_i=(1/N)Σ(∂̂y∂̂yᵀ+λ∂̂u∂̂uᵀ) and γ_i≤1. About 5–20 iterations; assumes an LTI loop. Extremum seeking (Killingsworth & Krstić 2005/2006). One step experiment per iteration, J_k=(1/(T−t0))∫e²dt. Update: ξ(k)=−hξ(k−1)+J(k−1); θ̂_i(k+1)=θ̂_i(k)−γ_iα_i cos(ω_i k)[J(k)−(1+h)ξ(k)]; θ_i(k+1)=θ̂_i(k+1)+α_i cos(ω_i(k+1)). Settings ω_i=aπ (a=0.8, offsets such as +π/2 between parameters), h=0.5, and plant-specific α_i (0.05–1) and γ_i (22–10000). Converged in <10 iterations on the paper's first plant and about 50 on others, starting from ZN. Bayesian optimisation (GP + EI/entropy search; Marco et al. 2016, Neumann-Brosig et al. 2020, SafeOpt by Berkenkamp et al. 2016): 20–50 evaluations, but each step costs an O(m³) GP fit, hyperparameter fitting and an inner acquisition optimisation.

**Why.** In Loop Tuner each evaluation costs about a millisecond, so their selling point (few or model-free experiments on hardware) does not apply. IFT's gradient trick assumes an LTI plant (it breaks for the tank, swing-up and saturation), and finite differences on the simulator give the same gradient more simply. Extremum seeking needs plant-specific α/γ, i.e. 'tuning the tuner'. Bayesian optimisation costs far more code than NM/CMA-ES for the same visible result. A 1–2 parameter Bayesian-optimisation demo with posterior mean and uncertainty could later be a nice teaching view.

## Pitfalls

- Relay on plants whose phase never reaches −180°: the DC motor speed loop is roughly first order, and the position loop k/(s(Ts+1)) only approaches −180° asymptotically. The limit cycle is then set by the ZOH or sampling delay (≈Ts/2), so Ku comes out huge and depends on Ts. Use a hysteresis relay, which moves the intersection to −180°+arcsin(ε/a); measure the point by DFT and use phase-margin placement instead of ZN; or use a step test.
- Integrating plants with relay: any bias error makes y drift and the half-periods unequal. Adapt the relay centre u0 every period, and start from the equilibrium input (for the tank: the outflow at the setpoint level). For integrating plants, an integral time that is too small causes slow oscillations; SIMC requires k'·Kc·Ti ≥ 4, hence Ti = 4(τc+θ).
- Unstable plants (cart with 1–3 rods): relay and open-loop step tests are impossible, and a direct search over K is almost entirely unstable. Use LQR weight search, abort diverging simulations early (angle > 90°, cart off the track), and grade the penalty by time to divergence so the optimiser can climb out.
- Saturation and windup: with actuator limits, IAE-optimal often becomes bang-bang and the optimiser exploits windup. Simulate the limits, implement anti-windup (back-calculation with Tt ≈ √(Ti·Td), or Ti for PI, or conditional integration), and penalise or limit time spent in saturation. Keep the relay amplitude d and step size inside the limits.
- Measurement noise: the derivative amplifies it. Evaluate with noise from a fixed seed (otherwise J is random and NM breaks), filter the derivative with Tf = Td/N (N = 5–20), penalise total variation of u, and set the relay hysteresis ε ≥ 2× the noise peak. Use DFT amplitudes rather than raw peak-to-peak.
- Overfitting to one reference signal, amplitude or time window: nonlinear plants (conical tank, pendulum, saturation) behave differently at other amplitudes and operating points. Always test r and d, optionally two amplitudes, plant variations of ±30 % (worst case), and the Ms bound. Validate on a held-out signal (ramp or sine) before applying.
- Finite horizon: with a 5 s window, slow tails after the window (too little integral action) cost nothing. Make the window ≥ 4–5 closed-loop time constants, or add ITAE or a terminal penalty |e(T)|. Make sure the r response has settled before the d step if both share one run.
- Non-smooth costs: overshoot, settling time, saturation and ZOH switching make the cost kinked, which stalls Nelder–Mead and breaks gradients. Keep integral metrics in the objective and event metrics as constraints with continuous hinge penalties. Restart NM when the simplex collapses. Use CMA-ES for multimodal problems such as swing-up.
- Scaling: search in log space and normalise cost terms to the start controller. Otherwise the weights mean nothing and the simplex degenerates.
- Parameterisation mix-ups: ideal/ISA (Kp, Ti, Td) vs parallel (Kp, Ki = Kp/Ti, Kd = Kp·Td) vs series (SIMC PID, with f = 1+Td/Ti). The rules assume the ideal form with a derivative filter and often a setpoint weight b; convert explicitly, and allow Ti = ∞ (no integral action).
- Sampling: continuous-time rules ignore the ZOH, so add Ts/2 to the delay L. If Ts is not much smaller than L or than the closed-loop time constant, design in discrete time.
- Describing-function error: the relay overestimates Ku by up to 27 % (4/π) for delay-dominated plants with square-wave output; it is accurate for lag-dominated plants. Use the first-harmonic DFT (Y1/U1) for an exact Nyquist point.
- Rule validity: ZN rules are aggressive (quarter-decay, Ms often 2–3). AMIGO frequency rules are invalid for κ < 0.2 (lag-dominant), and the AMIGO PID step rule is conservative for small τ. Cohen–Coon is oscillatory. Present rules as candidates, not as 'the answer'.
- Operating-point dependence: one relay or tuning run at one level does not carry over to the conical tank, whose gain and time constant change strongly with level. Tune at several levels and fill the gain-schedule table.
- LQR margin guarantees (GM ∈ [1/2,∞), PM ≥ 60°) hold only for continuous-time state feedback with full state measurement. Discretisation, observers and delays erode them (Doyle 1978, 'there are none' for LQG), so still check robustness.
- Reproducibility: seed every random number generator, record every evaluated parameter set with its cost, and apply exactly the simulated best. Re-simulating the best must reproduce the displayed curve.
